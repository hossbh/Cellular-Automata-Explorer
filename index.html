Learn: how cellular automata work

What is a cellular automaton?

A grid of cells, each in some state. At every tick of a clock, every cell looks at its neighbourhood and applies the same rule to decide its next state.

Nothing is global: no cell knows the overall shape. The shapes you see are emergent, built only from local interactions.

The seven ingredients

  1. Lattice: the grid (a row, a square, a cube). Space settings.
  2. States: on/off, several colours, or a real number (Continuous mode).
  3. Neighbourhood: Moore, von Neumann, a range r, or a smooth kernel.
  4. Rule: how the neighbourhood decides the next state.
  5. Update scheme: all cells at once, or a random fraction p.
  6. Boundary: wrapped edges (a torus) or a fixed empty border.
  7. Initial state: a seed, random noise, or your own drawing.

Reading a rule string

B lists the live-neighbour counts at which an empty cell is born; S lists the counts at which a live cell survives. Conway's Life is B3/S23.

C is the number of states. With C > 2, a cell that fails to survive decays through C − 2 dying states before it is empty again. Dying cells don't count as live neighbours. M and VN name the neighbourhood.

Neighbourhoods

Moore: every cell that touches the centre, even at a corner (8 in 2-D, 26 in 3-D).

von Neumann: only the cells that share a face (4 in 2-D, 6 in 3-D). Growth then spreads as a diamond or octahedron, because distance is counted as |x| + |y| + |z|.

Time as a dimension

In 2-D + time, each generation is a layer stacked on the one before. A still life becomes a straight column, an oscillator a braided column and a glider a slanted tube.

In 1-D + time, each row is a generation, as in Wolfram's space-time diagrams. Wrap the row onto a cylinder to see that the edges really are joined.

Four classes of behaviour

  1. Uniform: everything dies or fills (Class 1).
  2. Periodic: settles into still or repeating structures (Class 2).
  3. Chaotic: random-looking forever (Class 3, e.g. Rule 30).
  4. Complex: localised structures that move and interact (Class 4, e.g. Rule 110 and Life).

This scheme, proposed by Stephen Wolfram, is descriptive, not exact. Fig. 2 makes a quick guess for the current run.

The edge of chaos: Langton's λ

λ is the fraction of all neighbourhood patterns that produce a live cell. Near 0, everything dies (Class 1); near 0.5, behaviour is chaotic (Class 3).

Complex, computation-like behaviour tends to sit in between, at the “edge of chaos”: Conway's Life has λ ≈ 0.273. Fig. 3 shows λ for the current rule.

Cyclic and excitable automata

In a cyclic automaton the κ colours form a loop. A cell advances to the next colour only when at least θ of its neighbours (within range r) already have it, so colours chase each other.

From random noise, debris gives way to rotating spirals. The Greenberg–Hastings variant models excitable media (heart muscle, nerve tissue, the BZ reaction): a resting cell fires when stimulated, then must recover.

Continuous automata

States become real numbers between 0 and 1. In Lenia each cell takes a weighted average over a smooth ring of neighbours (the kernel), passes it through a growth function, and changes by a small time step dt. Lifelike “creatures” such as Orbium glide across the grid.

Reaction–diffusion (Gray–Scott) is another continuous automaton: two chemicals spread and react, making spots, stripes and dividing cells, as Turing proposed for animal markings.

Update schemes

The classic rule is synchronous: every cell reads the old grid, then all change at once. Set the update probability p below 1 and only a random fraction of cells updates each step (stochastic, or asynchronous, updating).

Many famous patterns, including oscillators, gliders and perfect symmetry, depend on synchrony and fall apart when p < 1.

Reversibility and information

Most rules forget: different grids can lead to the same next grid, so the past cannot be recovered, and some grids (“Gardens of Eden”) have no past at all.

A second-order rule makes any rule reversible: new = rule(current) XOR previous. In 1-D mode, tick Reversible, run, then press Reverse to watch history unwind.

Further concepts (explained, not simulated)

Other lattices: hexagonal and triangular grids, or cells on any network. Computation: Rule 110 and Life are Turing-complete, and Wireworld builds logic gates from 4 states.

Self-reproduction: von Neumann's 29-state constructor and Langton's loops copy themselves. Probabilistic rules model forest fires and epidemics.
